About

Decoding the genetics of infectious disease severity

Infectious disease outcomes emerge from the interaction of two genomes at once: the host's and the pathogen's. Studying either in isolation misses the genotype-by-genotype effects that often determine who develops severe disease. My work applies statistical genomics, population genetics, and machine learning to large-scale genomic and clinical datasets to identify the host and pathogen variants, and their interactions, that drive susceptibility and severity.

🧬

Statistical Genomics & GWAS

I run GWAS, case–control association analysis, and genetic risk score frameworks on large genomic datasets, using population structure correction, PCA, cross-validation, bootstrapping, and permutation testing to identify robust host genetic associations.

🦠

Host–Pathogen & Infection Genomics

I study how host genetic variation, particularly in immune-associated loci such as the HLA region, interacts with pathogen genomic variation to shape disease outcomes — work that spans comparative pathogen genomics, viral genome analysis, and virus–host association testing.

🤖

Machine Learning for Disease Risk

I build calibrated machine learning models — gradient boosting, random forests, neural networks — that integrate genomic variation with clinical and phenotypic data to model disease susceptibility and interpret complex genotype–phenotype relationships.

⚙️

Reproducible & Scalable Workflows

I develop reproducible bioinformatics pipelines using Snakemake and Nextflow, deployed in Linux/HPC (SLURM) and cloud (GCP) environments, with Git, Conda, and Docker for fully reproducible computational research.

🎯 Applying to MIVEGEC, CNRS

I am applying for the PhD position in Statistical Genomics in the group of Dr. Vincent Pedergnana at MIVEGEC (CNRS), Montpellier, focused on host–pathogen genome-to-genome (G×G) interactions in dengue virus disease severity. The project uses G×G regression models, mixed-effects and Bayesian approaches, and viral GWAS across paired human and dengue viral genomic data from 1,400 patients to identify how interactions at the human HLA region and viral proteins such as NS3 and NS5 shape clinical outcomes. This directly extends the host–pathogen genomics I began with HLA-region association testing during my bachelor's thesis, and the statistical genomics and machine-learning integration I have since built through my GWAS, PRS, and predictive modelling work. I am especially drawn to MIVEGEC's interdisciplinary environment, where statistical genomics, evolutionary genetics, and infectious disease biology converge to translate genomic variation into biological mechanism.
